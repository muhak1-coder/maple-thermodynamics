## Choose the model before substituting numbers
| Evidence in the question | Model or reasoning | Check first |
|---|---|---|
| Single phase changes temperature | Q=mcΔT | Correct phase, c, mass unit and final-minus-initial |
| Pure sample changes phase at transition conditions | Q=±mL | Correct transition and amount that actually changes |
| Several stages | One energy term per stage | Finish a stage before spending remaining energy |
| Time-temperature data with constant net power | Slope=ΔT/Δt; Q=P_netΔt | Axis units; seconds for W×time |
| Pressure-temperature diagram | Regions, boundaries and paths | Substance-specific diagram; equilibrium model |
| Gas with P,V,n,T | PV=nRT | Kelvin, absolute pressure, matching R and ideal approximation |

## Seven conceptual checkpoints
1. Solid particles vibrate, liquid particles rearrange among close neighbors, and gas particles spread through available space. A state change need not change chemical identity or mass.
2. Melting, vaporization and sublimation absorb heat in the usual thermal transition model. Freezing, condensation and deposition release it. Evaporation can happen below boiling.
3. A plateau can receive or release energy while temperature stays constant. Its duration is not itself a material constant.
4. ΔT is a change, not a final reading. Specific heat and conductivity answer different physical questions.
5. Compare the available heat with transition requirements before deciding the final phase. Ice at 0 °C need not be melted.
6. A phase boundary permits coexistence but does not specify the mass fractions. Triple and critical points are different.
7. Gas-law ratios need Kelvin. Moles count entities; grams measure mass. A gauge reading is not absolute pressure.

## Competition-style explanation routine
State the model, identify the relevant evidence, show the equation or graph path, and check the physical result. A correct number with the wrong model is fragile knowledge. For a drawing, label axes or system boundaries and say what the picture leaves out.
