# U2-C04 - Sensible heat

Unit 2 | All competition levels | public-student | Rule alignment: 5.c.ii, printed B57

## Learning objectives
Choose and rearrange Q=mcΔT for a single phase; keep units and signs consistent; separate heat received by water from energy supplied to a whole setup.

## Prerequisites
U1-C05–06 conversions, U2-C03 graph segments, elementary one-variable algebra.

## Start with two cups
Imagine equal-temperature cups holding different masses of the same liquid. Suppose both receive the same heat while remaining liquid. The smaller mass usually changes temperature more. The same energy is shared among less material. Now imagine equal masses of water and another substance. Their temperature changes can differ because materials require different energy amounts per mass per degree.

Specific heat c expresses this response. A large c means a relatively large heat input is needed to raise a given mass by one degree under stated conditions. Specific heat is not thermal conductivity: c concerns energy storage associated with temperature change, while conductivity concerns transfer through a material. Unit 3 will develop that difference.

“Sensible heat” refers to heat associated with a temperature change rather than a phase change. The name does not mean that feeling an object gives a safe or accurate temperature measurement. In this chapter, the useful model is Q=mcΔT. We use it for a specified single phase with c treated as approximately constant over the interval and no extra work or reactions requiring a different balance.

## Build the equation from meaning
Suppose a material needs c joules per gram for a 1 °C rise. A mass m grams then needs m×c joules for that same rise. A rise ΔT degrees multiplies that amount again. This gives Q=mcΔT, not because a formula must be memorized first, but because energy scales with mass and temperature change in the stated model.

Water near ordinary classroom temperatures is often modeled with c=4.18 J/(g·°C), equivalent to 4180 J/(kg·K). These are the same approximate specific heat in different units. If you use grams, choose the first form; if kilograms, the second. The 4.18 value is a rounded material property, not an exact conversion constant. Specific heat can vary with temperature and phase, so always use the value supplied in the problem.

The dimensions provide a check: g×J/(g·°C)×°C leaves J. A Celsius temperature change and a Kelvin temperature change have the same numerical size, so either works when paired with the corresponding c units. Absolute temperatures are different: do not replace ΔT with the final Kelvin reading.

## Decide the sign before computing
Define Q as positive for heat entering the selected sample. Define ΔT=T_final−T_initial. Warming gives a positive ΔT and positive Q in this model. Cooling gives both negative. If a problem asks “how much heat is released,” it often wants the positive magnitude |Q|; make clear that the sample's signed heat is negative.

For example, cooling from 40 °C to 25 °C means ΔT=−15 °C. Using +15 without saying it is a magnitude can reverse the physical meaning. A complete explanation states either “Q=−… J for the water” or “the water releases … J,” not an unexplained negative number.

## Solve for the requested variable
To solve for temperature change, divide both sides of Q=mcΔT by mc: ΔT=Q/(mc). Then T_final=T_initial+ΔT. To solve for mass, divide by cΔT: m=Q/(cΔT). To find a specific heat from suitable data, c=Q/(mΔT). Rearranging one equation for one variable is within the competition mathematics limits.

Before trusting the result, check that the assumed phase remains possible. If an assumed liquid-water calculation at ordinary pressure predicts a temperature far above boiling without accounting for vaporization, the model has crossed its boundary of validity. Split the process into stages instead of treating a phase transition as an ordinary rise.

## Keep the surroundings in view
In a real heating setup, energy can warm the cup, device parts and air as well as the water. Energy can also leave the system during heating. Q_water=mcΔT describes the heat needed for the stated water change under the model; it does not prove that a lamp supplied only that much energy. A device prediction based on perfect absorption is generally an optimistic model, not a validated prediction.

For several single-phase stages, calculate each stage with the correct phase's c. For a transition, use latent heat as taught next. These calculations remain arithmetic: identify a stage, choose its model, compute, and add signed transfers or positive required amounts consistently.

## Definitions and units
Specific heat c: energy per mass per temperature-change unit, J/(g·°C) or J/(kg·K). Sensible heat Q: heat associated with temperature change under stated conditions, J. Temperature change ΔT: final minus initial, °C or K. Heat capacity of the sample C=mc, in J/°C or J/K.

## Equations and their meaning
Q=mcΔT; ΔT=Q/(mc); T_f=T_i+ΔT; m=Q/(cΔT); c=Q/(mΔT). Use a constant-c single-phase model. Positive Q enters the sample. For water use c=4.18 J/(g·°C) unless the problem supplies another value.

## Worked example: Warm a water sample

**Given:** m=150 g; c=4.18 J/(g·°C); water warms from 18.0 °C to 28.0 °C with no phase change.

**Find:** Q into water.

**Equation:** Q=mc(T_f−T_i).

**Substitution:** Q=150 g×4.18 J/(g·°C)×(28.0−18.0) °C.

**Arithmetic:** ΔT=10.0 °C; 150×4.18×10.0=6270.

**Answer with units:** Q=+6.27×10³ J, or +6.27 kJ.

**Physical interpretation:** This is the modeled water requirement, not necessarily the lamp’s total output.

## Worked example: Cooling and a negative sign

**Given:** m=80.0 g of a material; c=0.900 J/(g·°C); it cools from 55.0 °C to 25.0 °C without phase change.

**Find:** Signed heat Q and heat-release magnitude.

**Equation:** Q=mcΔT.

**Substitution:** Q=80.0×0.900×(25.0−55.0) J.

**Arithmetic:** ΔT=−30.0 °C; 80.0×0.900×(−30.0)=−2160.

**Answer with units:** Q=−2.16×10³ J; 2.16 kJ is released.

**Physical interpretation:** The negative sign refers to energy leaving the selected sample.

## Worked example: Predict a final temperature

**Given:** A 100 g liquid-water sample begins at 21.0 °C and receives 2090 J; c=4.18 J/(g·°C); no phase change or other energy terms are included.

**Find:** Final water temperature.

**Equation:** ΔT=Q/(mc); T_f=T_i+ΔT.

**Substitution:** ΔT=2090/(100×4.18) °C; T_f=21.0+ΔT.

**Arithmetic:** 100×4.18=418; 2090/418=5.00; 21.0+5.00=26.0.

**Answer with units:** T_f=26.0 °C.

**Physical interpretation:** The modest rise is consistent with liquid water and the stated energy input.

## Common confusions
Use final minus initial, not final temperature alone. Convert g and kg consistently. A high c does not automatically mean slow heat transfer. Q=mcΔT is not the entire story when a sample changes phase.

## Real-life connection
For the event’s 100 mL water, an approximate mass of 100 g may be useful when a problem explicitly permits water density near 1.00 g/mL. Final temperature still depends on how much energy the water actually receives.

## Recap
Sensible-heat calculations connect mass, material response and temperature change. The equation works only with consistent units, a stated sign convention and an appropriate single-phase model.

## Review questions
1. Why does twice the mass need twice the heat for the same rise, if c is unchanged?
2. How does a negative Q differ from a negative temperature?
3. What additional calculation is needed after obtaining ΔT if the question asks for T_final?
