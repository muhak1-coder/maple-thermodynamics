# U2-C05 - Latent heat and multistage changes

Unit 2 | All competition levels | public-student | Rule alignment: 5.c.ii, printed B57

## Learning objectives
Use Q=mL for phase transitions; determine partial melting; combine sensible and latent stages without skipping boundaries.

## Prerequisites
U2-C02–04; mass conversion, energy conservation and one-variable rearrangement.

## Energy can change phase instead of temperature
Imagine ice and water coexisting at the melting temperature under a fixed pressure. Add a small amount of heat. Some ice melts, but the common equilibrium temperature can remain the same. The energy has not vanished. It changes the particle arrangement and interaction energy and, depending on the process, can also be associated with volume-change work. Heat during a transition should not automatically be identified with internal-energy change alone.

Latent heat L tells us the heat required per unit mass for a specified phase transition at stated conditions. The term “latent” historically suggests hidden: the energy transfer is not revealed by a temperature increase during the ideal plateau. It is not energy that has become impossible to measure. The melted or vaporized mass is another observable consequence.

For a complete transition of mass m, the heat magnitude is mL. We take tabulated L as positive. Melting, vaporization and sublimation require positive Q into the sample when driven thermally; freezing, condensation and deposition have negative Q for the same amount transformed under reverse conditions. If the question asks for the amount released, report the positive magnitude and state its direction.

## Fusion and vaporization are different
The latent heat of fusion L_f concerns melting/freezing. Fusion here means melting, not nuclear fusion. The latent heat of vaporization L_v concerns liquid-vapor conversion. For water near the specified ordinary-pressure transitions, use L_f=334 J/g at 0 °C and L_v=2260 J/g at 100 °C in this packet. These are rounded teaching values, not universal constants valid at all pressures and temperatures.

A gram of boiling water requires much more energy to become vapor than a gram of melting ice requires to become liquid. The number 2260 J/g is about 6.77 times 334 J/g. Do not assume every phase change uses the same L, and do not use ice's specific heat in a melting plateau just because ice is present.

## Partial transition: compare the budget first
If 10 g of ice at its melting temperature needs 3340 J to melt completely, an input smaller than 3340 J cannot melt it all under the ideal model. The melted mass is m_melted=Q/L_f. The remaining mass is the initial ice mass minus that melted mass. While both phases remain at equilibrium under the same pressure, the temperature stays at the melting temperature.

If the heat budget is larger than the required melting energy, first subtract the melting requirement. Only the remaining energy can warm the newly melted liquid. This order prevents a common mistake: using all the input energy to calculate a rise even though some had to change phase. If the starting ice is below the melting temperature, it must first be warmed using its own specific heat.

## Build a multistage energy ledger
For ice initially below 0 °C that finishes as liquid water above 0 °C at ordinary pressure, there are three stages: warm the ice, melt it, then warm the liquid. Write one line per stage. Use c_ice for the first, L_f for the second and c_water for the third. Add the positive heat requirements if the whole process is heating.

If the final state is vapor above the boiling temperature, add two more stages: vaporize the liquid and warm the vapor. Do not apply a single c across ice, liquid and gas. A question will supply the necessary material values. The long problem is a sequence of simple operations, not a demand for calculus.

For a cooling process, the stage order reverses and Q values are negative for energy leaving the sample. One may instead add release magnitudes, but the chosen convention must be clear. Mixing signed heats and positive magnitudes without labeling them can create an impossible net result.

## What the model assumes
The neat plateau applies to an idealized pure substance at fixed pressure near equilibrium. A solution or mixture may melt or boil over a range, and real specimens can have temperature gradients. Latent heat depends on conditions. In a classroom heating model, assume no unaccounted energy absorbed by the container and no loss unless the problem gives those terms. In a real device, those assumptions need evidence.

Water's latent heat explains why phase changes can dominate an energy budget. It also explains why a temperature trace alone cannot reveal every transfer: a constant temperature can accompany a large heat flow. However, the competition heat-collection activity is not an invitation to create steam or add ice or other substances to the supervisor's water. These examples are written-test models, not device-construction instructions.

## Definitions and units
Latent heat L: transition heat per mass, J/g or J/kg. L_f: latent heat of fusion. L_v: latent heat of vaporization. Partial melting: only part of the solid becomes liquid. Energy budget: available heat compared with the amounts required by successive stages.

## Equations and their meaning
For a transition, |Q|=mL. For heating transitions Q=+mL; for reverse transitions Q=−mL. m_changed=|Q|/L when all the specified transfer drives that transition. For successive heating stages Q_total=Q_1+Q_2+…; subtract completed-stage requirements before using the remaining budget.

## Worked example: Melt ice already at 0 °C

**Given:** 20.0 g ice at 0 °C melts to water at 0 °C near 1 atm; L_f=334 J/g.

**Find:** Required heat.

**Equation:** Q=mL_f.

**Substitution:** Q=20.0 g×334 J/g.

**Arithmetic:** 20.0×334=6680.

**Answer with units:** Q=+6.68 kJ.

**Physical interpretation:** Temperature does not change; all the specified heat drives melting.

## Worked example: Only part of the ice melts

**Given:** An ideal 40.0 g ice sample begins at 0 °C and receives 5010 J; L_f=334 J/g; no other heat sinks.

**Find:** Melted mass, remaining ice and final temperature.

**Equation:** m_melted=Q/L_f; m_remaining=m_initial−m_melted.

**Substitution:** m_melted=5010/334 g; m_remaining=40.0−m_melted.

**Arithmetic:** 5010/334=15.0; 40.0−15.0=25.0.

**Answer with units:** 15.0 g liquid and 25.0 g ice at 0 °C.

**Physical interpretation:** The full-melting requirement is 40.0×334=13,360 J, greater than the input. Two phases remain.

## Worked example: Warm ice, melt it, then warm the water

**Given:** 10.0 g ice at −10.0 °C finishes as liquid at 20.0 °C near 1 atm; c_ice=2.10 J/(g·°C), L_f=334 J/g, c_water=4.18 J/(g·°C).

**Find:** Total required heat, ignoring container and losses.

**Equation:** Q_total=mc_ice(0−T_i)+mL_f+mc_water(T_f−0).

**Substitution:** Q_1=10.0×2.10×10.0; Q_2=10.0×334; Q_3=10.0×4.18×20.0, all in J.

**Arithmetic:** Q_1=210 J; Q_2=3340 J; Q_3=836 J; sum=4386 J.

**Answer with units:** Q_total≈4.39 kJ.

**Physical interpretation:** The melting stage dominates. Treating the entire 30 °C span with liquid-water c would miss the transition.

## Common confusions
ΔT=0 does not imply Q=0 during a phase transition. L is not c and its units do not include a temperature denominator. “At the melting temperature” does not mean “already melted.” Use the phase fraction to check the final state.

## Real-life connection
Phase-change packs can absorb or release energy with modest temperature variation while a transition is underway. That principle is a thermodynamic connection, not a recommendation to add prohibited materials or energy sources to an event device.

## Recap
Choose L for the transition and c for temperature-changing stages. Compare the energy budget with each stage requirement before deciding the final phase and temperature.

## Review questions
1. Why does a latent-heat unit lack “per degree”?
2. What must happen before ice at −5 °C can begin melting near 1 atm?
3. If some ice remains after heating at equilibrium near 1 atm, what does that tell you about the final temperature?
