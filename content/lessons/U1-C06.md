# U1-C06 - Heat-energy units and numerical conversions

Unit 1 | All competition levels | public-student

## Learning objectives
Convert heat-related energy units; distinguish power from energy; use unit cancellation to check a calculation.

## Prerequisites
Decimal multiplication/division, ratios, minutes-to-seconds conversion, Unit 1 temperature distinctions.

## Learn the idea
Energy can be measured in different units just as a length can be measured in meters or centimeters. The standard SI energy unit is the joule, J. A kilojoule is 1000 J. A number becomes smaller when you rewrite the same energy in a larger unit: 2500 J is 2.5 kJ. The physical energy has not changed.

A small calorie, cal, is another energy unit. In this packet we use the thermochemical calorie: 1 cal=4.184 J exactly. Historical definitions of calorie differ slightly, so a problem should identify the convention when it matters. A kilocalorie is 1000 cal. A food-label Calorie, capital C, commonly means a kilocalorie: 1 Cal=1 kcal=4184 J. Confusing cal with Cal creates a factor-of-1000 error.

The name “calorie” connects to heating water, but an energy unit is not restricted to one material or transfer mechanism. Joules and calories can measure the same transferred energy. A temperature is still a different quantity: there is no universal conversion from joules to degrees Celsius. How much a sample's temperature changes depends on its mass, material and whether a phase change occurs.

Power is the rate at which energy is transferred or converted. One watt is one joule per second. A 5 W transfer delivers 5 J each second. Energy accumulates over time, so E=Pt when power is constant. Use seconds if power is in W and energy is wanted in J. A watt-hour is therefore energy, not power: 1 Wh=3600 J.

A useful unit-cancellation method is to multiply by a fraction equal to one. To convert 3.2 kJ to J, multiply 3.2 kJ by (1000 J)/(1 kJ). The kJ cancels, leaving J. For the opposite conversion, invert the factor. The units show whether you chose the direction correctly before you trust the arithmetic.

The energy emitted or supplied by a lamp is not necessarily the energy absorbed by the water. Some energy misses the device, is reflected away, heats other objects, or leaves to the surroundings. The device unit will build energy balances that include those paths. Never infer the water's energy gain simply by multiplying the bulb's rated electrical power by time and assuming perfect transfer.

For significant figures, conversion constants do not add precision to the measured value. Keep a few extra digits during arithmetic and round the final result to the precision requested by the question. A full answer includes a number, a unit, and a sentence checking whether its size is reasonable.

## Definitions and units
Joule J: energy unit. Kilojoule kJ: 1000 J. Thermochemical calorie cal: 4.184 J. Kilocalorie kcal: 4184 J. Food Calorie Cal: kcal. Watt W: J/s, a power unit. Watt-hour Wh: energy transferred at 1 W for 1 hour.

## Equations and their meaning
1 kJ=1000 J; 1 cal=4.184 J; 1 kcal=1000 cal=4184 J; 1 W=1 J/s; E=Pt at constant power; 1 Wh=3600 J. E is energy, P is power, t is elapsed time.

## Worked example 1: Calories to joules

**Given:** Energy transfer is 25.0 cal; use 4.184 J/cal.

**Find:** Energy in J.

**Equation:** E_J=E_cal×4.184 J/cal.

**Substitution:** E_J=25.0 cal×4.184 J/cal.

**Arithmetic:** 25.0×4.184=104.6.

**Answer with units:** 105 J to three significant figures.

**Physical interpretation:** The energy is the same; the numerical value is larger because a joule is smaller than a calorie.

## Worked example 2: Energy from constant power

**Given:** An illustrative transfer provides 6.0 W for 3.0 min.

**Find:** Energy transferred in J and kJ.

**Equation:** E=Pt; 1 min=60 s.

**Substitution:** t=3.0×60=180 s; E=6.0 J/s×180 s.

**Arithmetic:** 6.0×180=1080 J; 1080/1000=1.080 kJ.

**Answer with units:** 1.1×10³ J, or 1.1 kJ, to two significant figures.

**Physical interpretation:** This is transferred energy under the stated constant-rate model, not automatically the energy absorbed by a particular water sample.

## Worked example 3: A food-label conversion

**Given:** A label reports 2.00 Cal.

**Find:** Equivalent energy in kJ.

**Equation:** 1 Cal=4.184 kJ.

**Substitution:** E=2.00 Cal×4.184 kJ/Cal.

**Arithmetic:** 2.00×4.184=8.368.

**Answer with units:** 8.37 kJ.

**Physical interpretation:** Capital-C Calories are kilocalories; using 4.184 J per Cal would be 1000 times too small.

## Common confusions
W is power; Wh is energy. A calorie is not a temperature. Always distinguish cal, kcal and Cal. Lamp power times time describes supplied energy, while water gain requires a transfer model.

## Real-life connection
Power and time help bound possible device energy input, but real heating predictions require measured or modeled absorption and losses. Unit cancellation prevents misleading comparisons between J, kJ and W.

## Recap
Convert with ratios and cancel units. Keep power separate from energy. Use seconds with W when calculating J. Energy-to-temperature problems need additional information.
