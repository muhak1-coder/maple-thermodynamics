# U1-C03 - Intensive and extensive properties

Unit 1 | All competition levels | public-student

## Learning objectives
Classify intensive and extensive properties; apply the split/combine test; use density and mass consistently.

## Prerequisites
Chapters U1-C01–02; division and multiplication; basic volume units.

## Learn the idea
Suppose you have a uniform sample of water and gently separate it into two equal portions without changing its temperature or pressure appreciably. Each portion has half the original mass and half the original volume. Its temperature is unchanged. This thought experiment distinguishes properties that scale with the amount of material from properties that do not.

An extensive property scales with system size when comparable portions of a homogeneous sample are combined. Mass, volume and total internal energy are extensive. An intensive property does not scale simply with the amount. Temperature, pressure and density are intensive. These labels describe how a property behaves under a controlled comparison; they do not mean that the property can never change.

For example, density can change with temperature or composition, but doubling an otherwise identical uniform sample does not double its density. Pressure can change if you compress a gas, but that does not make pressure extensive. The split/combine thought experiment must preserve the relevant physical conditions. Squeezing twice as much gas into the same volume is not the same experiment as combining two identical portions while allowing volume to double.

Density is mass per volume: ρ=m/V. For two identical portions, both numerator and denominator double. Their ratio stays the same. That is why a ratio of two extensive properties can be intensive. Specific heat will later provide another example: heat capacity grows with sample mass, but heat capacity per mass is a material property under stated conditions.

To picture this, draw one rectangle labeled “80 g, 80 mL, 25 °C” and two half-sized rectangles labeled “40 g, 40 mL, 25 °C.” Keep the temperature label unchanged. Do not split the reading into 12.5 °C. This drawing assumes uniform water and negligible exchange during separation.

Internal energy comparisons require care. For identical material in the same phase, temperature and other relevant conditions, adding an identical portion adds its internal energy. But mass alone cannot compare unlike objects at different conditions. We also choose consistent energy reference states when quoting U. For beginner questions, use explicit ratios or stated illustrative energy values rather than assuming that every object's internal energy is a known number.

Heat itself is not a state property of the material. The transferred amount often scales with mass for the same process, but “heat in the cup” should not appear on a list of stored system properties.

## Definitions and units
Extensive property: scales with amount under comparable conditions. Intensive property: does not scale with amount. Homogeneous: uniform composition and properties on the scale considered. Density ρ: mass divided by volume; commonly kg/m³ or g/mL.

## Equations and their meaning
ρ=m/V; m=ρV; V=m/ρ. In comparisons at the same state, doubling a uniform sample doubles mass and volume but leaves density unchanged. 1 mL = 1 cm³.

## Worked example 1: Calculate an intensive property

**Given:** A uniform liquid has mass 72 g and volume 80 mL.

**Find:** Density.

**Equation:** ρ=m/V.

**Substitution:** ρ=72 g ÷ 80 mL.

**Arithmetic:** 72 ÷ 80 = 0.90.

**Answer with units:** 0.90 g/mL.

**Physical interpretation:** Each mL has an average mass of 0.90 g under these conditions.

## Worked example 2: Combine identical portions

**Given:** Two portions each have mass 45 g, volume 50 mL and temperature 24 °C.

**Find:** Combined mass, volume, density and temperature, with no transfer or mixing effects.

**Equation:** m_total=m_1+m_2; V_total=V_1+V_2; ρ=m_total/V_total.

**Substitution:** m_total=45+45 g; V_total=50+50 mL; ρ=90/100 g/mL.

**Arithmetic:** Totals are 90 g and 100 mL; 90/100=0.90.

**Answer with units:** 90 g; 100 mL; 0.90 g/mL; 24 °C.

**Physical interpretation:** The extensive quantities doubled. Density and temperature did not.

## Common confusions
Intensive does not mean intense, large or fixed forever. Temperature readings do not add when identical samples combine. Total internal energy and temperature are different properties. Density is not the same as mass.

## Real-life connection
A small and a large aluminum part can have the same density but very different masses. In device testing, doubling the amount of water changes the thermal task even if its initial temperature is unchanged.

## Recap
Use a controlled split/combine comparison. Mass, volume and internal energy scale with amount; temperature, pressure and density do not simply scale with amount.
