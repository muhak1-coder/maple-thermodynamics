# U2-C07 - Ideal gas law and units

Unit 2 | All competition levels | public-student | Rule alignment: 5.c.ii, printed B57

## Learning objectives
Explain the particle basis of PV=nRT; choose consistent pressure, volume and temperature units; solve for one unknown and test whether the ideal model is appropriate.

## Prerequisites
Unit 1 Kelvin conversion and algebra; U2-C01 particles and U2-C06 phase conditions.

## From collisions to a useful equation
A gas exerts pressure because its particles collide with the walls of the container. Add more particles to the same volume at the same temperature and collisions generally become more frequent, raising the pressure. Heat a fixed gas sample at fixed volume and its particles transfer more momentum in collisions on average, also raising pressure. Give that fixed sample more volume at the same temperature and the pressure falls.

The ideal gas law combines these relationships: PV=nRT. Pressure P and volume V describe the container-scale state. Temperature T describes the thermal state and must be absolute, in Kelvin. The amount n tells us how many particles are present in moles. R is the gas constant connecting the chosen units. The equation is a model of a gas, not an equation for liquid water or ice.

An ideal gas treats particles as having negligible volume relative to the container and negligible interactions except ideal elastic collisions. Real gases often behave approximately ideally when relatively dilute and well away from condensation. High pressure, low temperature near a phase boundary, and strong intermolecular effects can make deviations important. The model is not equally accurate under all conditions, and the equation alone cannot prove a gas will remain a gas during a large change.

## A mole counts particles, not grams
A mole is a counting unit, like a dozen but enormously larger. One mole contains exactly 6.02214076×10²³ specified entities. In ordinary problems, the problem may simply give n. Equal numbers of moles of different gases contain equal numbers of the specified gas particles, but their masses need not match.

If mass and molar mass are provided, n=m/M. Here M is mass per mole, such as g/mol. Thus 4.00 g of a gas with M=4.00 g/mol is 1.00 mol; 4.00 g of a gas with M=40.0 g/mol is 0.100 mol. Do not insert grams directly where PV=nRT asks for moles. No periodic-table memorization is needed when the problem supplies M.

## Select one internally consistent unit set
Use R=8.314 kPa·L/(mol·K) with P in kPa and V in L. The same numerical R=8.314 J/(mol·K) works with P in Pa and V in m³ because 1 Pa·m³=1 J. It also works because 1 kPa·L=1 J. Another common version is R=0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K), paired with atmospheres and liters. Never combine a constant from one row with pressure/volume units from another without conversion.

Useful conversions are 1 kPa=1000 Pa, 1 L=0.001 m³ and 1 atm=101.325 kPa. Check that both sides of the equation have matching units. With kPa and L, nRT has units mol×kPa·L/(mol·K)×K=kPa·L, matching PV. This dimensional check often catches a factor-of-1000 mistake.

Use absolute pressure, measured relative to vacuum. A gauge pressure is relative to surrounding atmospheric pressure. If a problem supplies gauge pressure, add the local atmospheric pressure to obtain absolute pressure. For example, a 20 kPa gauge reading in 100 kPa surroundings means 120 kPa absolute. A sealed gas container at room pressure does not have zero absolute pressure simply because a gauge reads zero.

## Kelvin is essential
The gas law relates temperature to an absolute zero. Convert with T_K=T_C+273.15. A gas at 20 °C is at 293.15 K. Doubling from 20 °C to 40 °C is not doubling absolute temperature, so it will not double pressure at fixed n and V. A change of 20 °C is a change of 20 K, but the ratio uses the absolute readings, not their difference.

To solve for P, divide by V: P=nRT/V. For V, divide by P: V=nRT/P. For n, divide by RT: n=PV/(RT). For T, divide by nR: T=PV/(nR), giving Kelvin if you used consistent units. A final conversion to Celsius may follow if requested. Show the rearrangement before substitution so the unknown's role is clear.

## Compare two states carefully
For a fixed amount of ideal gas, PV/T is constant. Therefore P_1V_1/T_1=P_2V_2/T_2. If V is fixed, pressure is proportional to Kelvin temperature. If T is fixed, pressure and volume change inversely. If P is fixed, volume is proportional to Kelvin temperature. Each shortcut needs the stated fixed quantities. A leaking balloon is not automatically a fixed-n system.

For Science Olympiad reasoning, first name what stays fixed, then predict the direction, then calculate. If a fixed-temperature gas is compressed to half its volume, the pressure should double in the model; a result that halves it signals an algebra error. If a calculated temperature or pressure is extreme, reconsider the ideal approximation and phase conditions rather than treating a calculator output as proof of physical feasibility.

## Definitions and units
P: absolute gas pressure, kPa or Pa or atm. V: gas volume, L or m³. n: amount of gas, mol. T: absolute temperature, K. R: gas constant with units matching P and V. M: molar mass, g/mol. Gauge pressure: pressure relative to local surroundings, not vacuum.

## Equations and their meaning
PV=nRT; P=nRT/V; V=nRT/P; n=PV/(RT); T=PV/(nR). n=m/M when mass and molar mass are supplied. For fixed n, P_1V_1/T_1=P_2V_2/T_2. Use absolute pressure and Kelvin readings. In this unit, R=8.314 kPa·L/(mol·K) unless otherwise stated.

## Worked example: Find pressure from amount, volume and temperature

**Given:** n=0.200 mol; V=5.00 L; T=300 K; R=8.314 kPa·L/(mol·K); ideal gas.

**Find:** Absolute pressure.

**Equation:** P=nRT/V.

**Substitution:** P=(0.200×8.314×300)/5.00 kPa.

**Arithmetic:** Numerator=498.84 kPa·L; 498.84/5.00=99.768.

**Answer with units:** P≈99.8 kPa.

**Physical interpretation:** The result is near ordinary atmospheric pressure; the units are consistent.

## Worked example: Find volume after converting Celsius

**Given:** n=0.100 mol; T=27.0 °C; P=100 kPa absolute; R=8.314 kPa·L/(mol·K).

**Find:** Gas volume.

**Equation:** T_K=T_C+273.15; V=nRT/P.

**Substitution:** T=300.15 K; V=(0.100×8.314×300.15)/100 L.

**Arithmetic:** 8.314×300.15=2495.4471; V=2.4954471.

**Answer with units:** V≈2.50 L to three significant figures.

**Physical interpretation:** Using 27 instead of 300.15 would greatly underestimate the volume.

## Worked example: A fixed-volume temperature comparison

**Given:** A sealed rigid vessel has ideal gas at 90.0 kPa absolute and 300 K; it warms to 330 K with unchanged n.

**Find:** Final absolute pressure.

**Equation:** P_2=P_1T_2/T_1 at fixed n,V.

**Substitution:** P_2=90.0×330/300 kPa.

**Arithmetic:** 330/300=1.10; 90.0×1.10=99.0.

**Answer with units:** P_2=99.0 kPa.

**Physical interpretation:** A 10% Kelvin-temperature increase gives a 10% pressure increase in the model.

## Common confusions
Use Kelvin readings, absolute pressure and moles. R changes its numerical form with units; it is not unitless. Fixed-mass comparisons fail if gas escapes. PV=nRT does not describe condensed phases or automatically account for vaporization.

## Real-life connection
Temperature changes affect gas pressure in sealed equipment and gas volume in flexible containers. These are written reasoning contexts here, not instructions to heat sealed vessels. Competition water itself is modeled as liquid, while the surrounding air is a gas.

## Recap
The ideal gas law combines particle amount, temperature, volume and absolute pressure. Unit consistency and model conditions are as important as arithmetic.
