# U1-C04 - Thermal equilibrium and zeroth law

Unit 1 | All competition levels | public-student

## Learning objectives
Use thermal equilibrium and the zeroth law; distinguish equilibrium from equal energy; explain how a thermometer obtains a useful reading.

## Prerequisites
Heat versus temperature; comparing temperatures; elementary subtraction.

## Learn the idea
Put a warm object beside a cooler object with a path for thermal energy transfer. Net heat transfer is initially from the warmer object to the cooler one. Over time their temperatures can approach each other. Thermal equilibrium means there is no net heat transfer between objects in thermal contact because they have the same temperature.

The word net matters. Microscopic motion and exchanges do not stop at equilibrium. Opposing transfers balance statistically. Thermal equilibrium also does not mean that every kind of physical change has ceased; chemical reactions or motion might still matter in another problem. We are discussing the thermal relationship.

The zeroth law makes temperature comparison consistent. If A is in thermal equilibrium with C, and B is also in thermal equilibrium with C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other. C acts as a common thermal reference. This is why a thermometer can help compare objects without making every pair touch directly.

A thermometer must interact with what it measures. It takes time for its sensing part to approach the sample's temperature. Reading it immediately can give a value between its old temperature and the sample temperature. Ideally the thermometer's effect on the sample is small; a large, very cold probe in a tiny warm sample can change the quantity being measured. A stable reading and appropriate measurement method matter.

Equal temperatures do not require equal masses or equal internal energies. A cup and a large tank of the same water can both be at 23 °C. They can be in thermal equilibrium even though the tank contains much more material. Nor does equal temperature tell you that both must be 0 °C: equilibrium can occur across many temperatures.

A special averaging shortcut is sometimes useful but must be labeled. If two equal masses of the same material in the same phase exchange energy only with each other, have approximately constant equal specific heats, and do no work, their common final temperature is the average of their initial temperatures. This is not a general rule for unequal masses, unlike materials, phase changes, or heat exchange with a container. In Unit 3, calorimetry will explain the shortcut from energy conservation.

An equilibrium diagram can show two objects marked with the same T and two opposing energy arrows of equal size. For a non-equilibrium diagram, use different T labels and a single arrow labeled “net heat transfer” pointing from high T toward low T.

## Definitions and units
Thermal equilibrium: equal-temperature condition giving no net heat exchange in thermal contact. Zeroth law: equilibrium through a common reference implies mutual thermal equilibrium. Thermometer response time: time needed for a sensor to approach the measured temperature.

## Equations and their meaning
Temperature difference: ΔT=T_high−T_low. Special equal-sample model only: T_final=(T_1+T_2)/2. The zeroth law is a relationship, not a numerical heat formula.

## Worked example 1: Use a common reference

**Given:** A is in equilibrium with a 26.0 °C reference; B is independently in equilibrium with the same unchanged reference.

**Find:** Temperature difference between A and B.

**Equation:** ΔT=T_A−T_B.

**Substitution:** ΔT=26.0 °C−26.0 °C.

**Arithmetic:** 26.0−26.0=0.0.

**Answer with units:** 0.0 °C difference.

**Physical interpretation:** The zeroth law predicts no net heat transfer when A and B are placed in thermal contact.

## Worked example 2: Equal-sample model

**Given:** Equal water masses start at 18.0 °C and 30.0 °C; same constant specific heat, no phase change, no work or environmental exchange, negligible container energy.

**Find:** Final common temperature.

**Equation:** T_final=(T_1+T_2)/2 for this special model.

**Substitution:** T_final=(18.0+30.0)/2 °C.

**Arithmetic:** 48.0/2=24.0.

**Answer with units:** 24.0 °C.

**Physical interpretation:** It lies between the starting temperatures. The averaging shortcut is justified here, not for every mixture.

## Common confusions
Thermal equilibrium does not mean equal internal energy, no particle motion, or zero temperature. A brief touch does not prove equilibrium has been reached. A maintained temperature difference with continuous energy flow is not mutual thermal equilibrium.

## Real-life connection
A classroom thermometer should settle before its value is recorded. In competition, the event supervisor controls the starting and final measurements; students work from those readings rather than adding their own probe.

## Recap
Thermal equilibrium is about equal temperature and zero net heat exchange. A common reference connects comparisons. Good measurement considers both response time and disturbance.
