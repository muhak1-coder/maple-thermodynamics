# U2-C06 - Reading phase diagrams

Unit 2 | All competition levels | public-student | Rule alignment: 5.c.ii, printed B57

## Learning objectives
Identify phase regions and coexistence boundaries; follow constant-pressure and constant-temperature paths; distinguish triple and critical points from an ordinary boiling point.

## Prerequisites
U2-C01–03; coordinate graphs, axis units, interpolation and temperature differences.

## A map of equilibrium states
A phase diagram tells you which phase is stable at a specified temperature and pressure. The horizontal axis is commonly temperature, and the vertical axis pressure. This is not a heating curve: neither axis is time, and no line automatically tells you how fast a sample warms. Read the axes before deciding what a movement across the page means.

Choose a point by locating its temperature horizontally and pressure vertically. If it lies inside a solid, liquid or gas region, that phase is stable in the equilibrium model. A point on a boundary represents conditions at which the adjacent phases can coexist. The diagram does not tell you how much of each phase is present there. To find phase amounts, you need additional information such as mass and an energy balance.

The original diagram here represents hypothetical substance X. Its numerical coordinates and straight-line connections are chosen for teaching and are not measurements for water or any identified chemical. Treat the boundary coordinates as the definition of this model. This avoids accidentally reading exact physical constants from a schematic sketch.

## Read the three coexistence boundaries
The solid-gas boundary separates solid from gas and describes sublimation/deposition equilibrium. The solid-liquid boundary describes melting/freezing equilibrium. The liquid-gas boundary describes boiling/condensation equilibrium. Moving across a boundary changes the stable phase under a sufficiently slow equilibrium process. The crossing direction determines the transition name.

All three boundaries meet at the triple point. At this unique temperature and pressure for a pure substance, solid, liquid and gas can coexist in equilibrium. “Triple” refers to three phases, not three different substances or three temperatures. Below the triple-point pressure in this ordinary single-component diagram, no stable liquid region is available. Heating a solid at such a pressure takes it directly into the gas region by sublimation.

The liquid-gas boundary ends at the critical point. Beyond the critical temperature and critical pressure, the fluid is supercritical: it lacks a distinct liquid-gas phase boundary of the usual kind. It is not correct to extend an ordinary boiling line forever or to claim that raising pressure alone above the critical temperature necessarily produces a separate liquid phase. The solid-liquid boundary shown need not end at the critical point; that point concerns the liquid-gas distinction.

## Follow a path deliberately
At constant pressure, move horizontally. At constant temperature, move vertically. For substance X at 110 kPa, warming from 10 °C to 90 °C begins in the solid region, crosses the melting boundary at 26 °C, passes through the liquid region and crosses the boiling boundary at 60 °C before ending as gas. The exact 26 °C crossing follows the stated straight solid-liquid line, not guesswork from an unlabeled image.

At a constant temperature of 40 °C, increasing pressure from 40 to 110 kPa crosses the liquid-gas boundary at 70 kPa. The stable state changes from gas to liquid. That process is condensation caused by a change of pressure under the model. The temperature can remain constant even though the state changes.

A point at a boundary is not automatically “in the higher-pressure phase.” Both adjacent phases are allowed at equilibrium. A path along a boundary is different from crossing it. State what the point or path does instead of memorizing “up means liquid,” because an upward path can cross different boundaries at different temperatures.

## Real substances need their own diagrams
For many substances, the melting line slopes toward higher temperature as pressure increases, corresponding to a solid denser than its liquid under the relevant conditions. Water near ordinary conditions is an important exception: ordinary ice is less dense than liquid water, and its melting line has a negative slope in the usual pressure-temperature plot. Do not use substance X's positive melting slope as a universal law.

The usual boiling temperature of water near 100 °C refers to approximately 1 atmosphere. On a phase diagram, a normal boiling point is the liquid-gas boundary crossing at 1 atmosphere, not the triple or critical point. Pressure changes the boiling temperature, and real phase diagrams can include additional solid forms at high pressure. This beginner model intentionally focuses on one solid, one liquid and one gas region.

## Read numbers without overclaiming
Use supplied values or clearly stated interpolation. The diagram's coordinate list accompanies the figure so an exact problem does not depend on measuring pixels. For a straight segment, proportional interpolation is allowed with elementary ratios. For a real curved boundary, do not assume linearity unless the problem permits an approximation.

A phase diagram supports equilibrium reasoning. It does not specify the heat required, the rate of transition, the amount of substance or the temperature lag of a real experiment. Combine it with other information only after naming the relevant assumptions.

## Definitions and units
Phase boundary: conditions allowing two adjacent phases to coexist. Triple point: equilibrium coexistence of solid, liquid and gas at a particular T and P. Critical point: endpoint of the liquid-gas coexistence curve. Normal boiling point: boiling temperature at 1 atm. Supercritical fluid: fluid beyond its critical temperature and pressure without a distinct ordinary liquid-gas boundary.

## Equations and their meaning
No universal equation generates every phase diagram. For the fictional X model only, the solid-liquid line is P=10T−150 with P in kPa and T in °C over the plotted segment 20≤T≤45. Rearrange to T=(P+150)/10. This is a teaching-line definition, not a physical law. Other boundary values are given in the diagram table.

## Worked example: A constant-pressure liquid range

**Given:** For X at 110 kPa, the melting line obeys P=10T−150 and the table gives boiling at 60 °C.

**Find:** Melting temperature and width of the stable-liquid temperature interval.

**Equation:** T_m=(P+150)/10; interval width=T_b−T_m.

**Substitution:** T_m=(110+150)/10; width=60−T_m.

**Arithmetic:** 260/10=26; 60−26=34.

**Answer with units:** Melting at 26 °C; liquid-region interval width 34 °C (between 26 and 60 °C).

**Physical interpretation:** At either boundary phases may coexist; strictly inside the interval the stable phase is liquid.

## Worked example: Read a vaporization boundary with stated interpolation

**Given:** The X boundary contains (60 °C,110 kPa) and (80 °C,170 kPa), joined by a straight segment in this model.

**Find:** Boundary pressure at 70 °C.

**Equation:** Fraction=(70−60)/(80−60); P=110+fraction×(170−110).

**Substitution:** Fraction=10/20; P=110+(10/20)×60 kPa.

**Arithmetic:** Fraction=0.5; P=110+30=140.

**Answer with units:** 140 kPa.

**Physical interpretation:** At (70 °C,140 kPa), liquid and vapor can coexist. This interpolation is justified only by the stated straight-segment model.

## Common confusions
A phase diagram is not a graph of temperature versus time. Boundary location does not determine phase fractions. Triple and critical points are different. Below triple-point pressure, the liquid region is absent in this model; this is not the same statement as being below the critical pressure.

## Real-life connection
Lower atmospheric pressure changes the boiling temperature, which can affect cooking. Pressure-temperature reasoning also explains why temperature alone may not identify a substance’s phase.

## Recap
Locate a coordinate, identify its region or boundary, then follow a stated path. Use the substance-specific diagram and keep phase equilibrium separate from heating rates and heat amounts.
